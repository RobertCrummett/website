Coordinate Transformations — First Order Systems of ODEs

I just learned a new trick to transform systems of ordinary differential equations in Cartesian coordinates into polar coordinates. It is kind of basic, but leads to interesting physical interpretations, so I will record it here.

As everyone knows, 𝑥=𝑟cos(𝜃) and 𝑦=𝑟sin(𝜃). We will use these formula below.

By definition 𝑟2=𝑥2+𝑦2. Taking the derivative with respect to time 𝑡,

2𝑟𝑟̇=2𝑥𝑥̇+2𝑦𝑦̇⇒𝑟𝑟̇=𝑥𝑥̇+𝑦𝑦̇.

A formula for 𝑟̇. We can derive a similar formula for 𝜃̇. Differentiate the definitions of 𝑥 and 𝑦 with respect to 𝑡:

𝑥̇=𝑟̇cos(𝜃)−𝑟𝜃̇sin(𝜃)𝑦̇=𝑟̇sin(𝜃)+𝑟𝜃̇cos(𝜃),

which follows from application of the product rule and chain rule. To isolate 𝜃̇, we use a technique. We multiply the equation for 𝑥̇ by a term proportional to sin(𝜃), and the equation for 𝑦̇ with a term proportional to cos(𝜃). We do this so we can apply the well known identity cos2(𝜃)+sin2(𝜃)=1.

𝑥̇𝑦=𝑟̇cos(𝜃)𝑟sin(𝜃)−𝑟2𝜃̇sin2(𝜃)𝑦̇𝑥=𝑟̇sin(𝜃)𝑟cos(𝜃)+𝑟2𝜃̇cos2(𝜃),

Now, subtracting the two expressions allows us to isolate 𝜃̇ with the identity:

𝑦̇𝑥−𝑥̇𝑦=𝑟̇sin(𝜃)𝑟cos(𝜃)+𝑟2𝜃̇cos2(𝜃)−(𝑟̇cos(𝜃)𝑟sin(𝜃)−𝑟2𝜃̇sin2(𝜃))=𝑟2𝜃̇cos2(𝜃)+𝑟2𝜃̇sin2(𝜃)=𝑟2𝜃̇(cos2(𝜃)+sin2(𝜃))=𝑟2𝜃̇.

Relatively simple combinations of the original expressions in terms of 𝑥, 𝑦, 𝑥̇, and 𝑦̇ yield simple expressions in polar coordinates. The term 𝑟2𝜃̇ bears similarity to the 𝑧-component of a cross product 𝑟⃗×𝑣⃗, where 𝑟⃗ is the position vector and 𝑣⃗ is the velocity vector. The term 𝑟𝑟̇ bears similarity to a dot product 𝑟⃗⋅𝑣⃗. These are fascinating similarities to me.
