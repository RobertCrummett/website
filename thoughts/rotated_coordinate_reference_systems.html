Rotated Coordinate Reference Systems

Prospect scale surveys are laid out relative to geology, not geography. Most survey grids end up rotated with resepct to true north. Processing is easier in a survey-aligned frame. Line-based filters such as decorrugation and microlevelling expect the lines to run along a grid axis. Directional derivatives along and across strike become plain 𝑥 and 𝑦 derivatives. A rectangular block wastes no padding in an FFT.

There is no “rotated UTM” in the EPSG registry, and GIS software cannot rotate a projected CRS for you. The standard workaround is the Hotine Oblique Mercator projection (EPSG method 9815, “variant B”). It is a conformal projection with two angles: one that rotates the projection and one that rotates the grid. This post explains the two angles, shows how to build the CRS on the command line with PROJ, and lists the traps I fell into.

The Two Angles

Hotine first projects the ellipsoid onto a cylinder that touches the earth along a great-circle-like initial line. The initial line passes through the projection center (𝜑𝑐,𝜆𝑐) at azimuth 𝛼𝑐. Scale is true along this line. The projection first produces skew coordinates (𝑢,𝑣), with 𝑢 along the initial line and 𝑣 90° clockwise from it. These are then rectified into eastings and northings by a plane rotation through 𝛾𝑐 [1]:

𝐸=𝑣cos𝛾𝑐+𝑢sin𝛾𝑐+𝐸𝑐𝑁=𝑢cos𝛾𝑐−𝑣sin𝛾𝑐+𝑁𝑐

The two parameters therefore do different jobs:

  • +alpha (𝛼𝑐, Azimuth of initial line) is a projection choice. It sets where the scale is true, so it controls distortion.
  • +gamma (𝛾𝑐, Angle from Rectified to Skew Grid) is a grid choice. It rotates the final (𝐸,𝑁) axes in the plane and has no effect on distortion.

Setting 𝛾𝑐=0 gives 𝑁=𝑢, so grid north lies along the initial line. Setting 𝛾𝑐=𝛼𝑐 rotates grid north back onto true north at the projection center [1]. This is the default PROJ uses when +gamma is omitted [2], and it is how national grids such as Alaska zone 1 are defined. Grid north at the center therefore has azimuth

𝜃=𝛼𝑐−𝛾𝑐.

By default, 𝛼𝑐=𝛾𝑐. In practice, choose 𝜃 to be the survey grid orientation, and 𝛼𝑐 to control the distortion.

Which 𝛼 to choose

For prospect scale work the choice hardly matters. With +k=1 and the center on the survey, the point scale factor 50 km off the initial line is about 1+𝑑2/2𝑅2≈1.00003, or 30 ppm. That is 3 mm across a 100 m cell. In the example below, a 20 km step along the initial line maps to exactly 20,000.00 m. A 20 km step perpendicular to it maps to 20,000.03 m.

𝛼𝑐 starts to matter when the survey is long and narrow, such as a regional strip or a corridor several hundred kilometres long. In that case run the initial line down the long axis. Hotine’s construction itself starts to break down several hundred kilometres from the center along the initial line [1]. Beyond that, use a different projection or several blocks.

Setting 𝛼𝑐=0 makes the initial line a meridian, so the projection is transverse Mercator. With PROJ 9.4, +proj=omerc +alpha=0 +gamma=0 centered on my test survey agreed with +proj=tmerc on the same center to 0.1 mm at 50 km. So +alpha=0 +gamma=-theta is effectively “transverse Mercator centered on the survey, rotated by 𝜃”. Easy to explain to colleagues.

Traps

Keep 𝛼 in [−90°,90°]. The formulas use 𝛾0=arcsin(sin𝛼𝑐/𝐷) [1]. Because arcsin only returns values in [−90°,90°], an azimuth outside that range is folded back into it. In my tests with PROJ 9.4 and 9.6, +alpha=120 gave exactly the same projection as +alpha=60, with true scale along the 60° line rather than the 120° line, and PROJ gave no warning. A line has two azimuths, so use the equivalent value 𝛼−180°: here, +alpha=-60.

Center the projection on the survey. You might try to imitate UTM by placing the center at the zone’s origin (latitude 0, central meridian, +alpha=0, +k=0.9996) and then rotating. Because Hotine passes through an intermediate sphere fitted at the center, this only matches UTM near the equator. At 10° S, my test differed from UTM by about 0.1 ppm in scale. At 40.5° N, the coordinates were off by about 250 m, and the scale factor was 0.99938 instead of UTM’s 0.99965, a 270 ppm difference. Put +lat_0 and +lonc in the middle of the block.

+no_rot means “no rectification”, not “no rotation” [2]. It returns the raw (𝑢,𝑣) skew coordinates. Almost never useful.

+no_off switches to variant A [2]. In variant A the false easting and northing are given at the natural origin, where the initial line crosses the equator of the intermediate sphere. That point can be thousands of kilometers away. Variant B, the default, puts (𝐸𝑐,𝑁𝑐) at the projection center, which is what you want.

The scale factor. +k=1 makes distances true at the center. A UTM grid is not true scale. At my test center, 63 km east of the central meridian, UTM’s point scale factor is 0.99965, a 350 ppm difference, or about 17 m over 50 km. If the rotated coordinates must match an existing UTM dataset in scale, set +k to UTM’s point scale factor at the center. You can read it from proj -V (see below).

Building One on the Command Line

Everything below uses the PROJ command-line tools (cs2cs, projinfo, proj, geod) and GDAL. The survey is invented: a block in UTM zone 11N (EPSG:32611) centerd at 563,550 E, 4,483,525 N, with flight lines at 120° and tie lines at 30°. I tested the commands with PROJ 9.4 and 9.6 and GDAL 3.10. On Windows, QGIS ships all of these tools: open the OSGeo4W Shell that comes with it. The snippets use POSIX shell syntax, so translate the variables and pipes if you run them in cmd.

1. Find the center in latitude and longitude

echo "563550.68 4483524.87" | cs2cs -d 6 EPSG:32611 EPSG:4326
# 40.500000  -116.250000   (lat lon!)

2. Write the PROJ string

Use 𝜃=30° and the transverse Mercator form (𝛼𝑐=0, so 𝛾𝑐=−30°). Choose a false origin at the center that keeps every coordinate positive and cannot be mistaken for UTM:

OMERC="+proj=omerc +lat_0=40.5 +lonc=-116.25 +alpha=0 +gamma=-30 +k=1 +x_0=500000 +y_0=1000000 +datum=WGS84 +units=m +no_defs +type=crs"

3. Check the axes before trusting it

Use geod to step 20 km from the center along the flight-line and tie-line azimuths, then project those points. A flight-line step should change only 𝑥, and a tie-line step only 𝑦:

printf "40.5 -116.25 120 20000\n40.5 -116.25 30 20000\n" \
  | geod +ellps=WGS84 -f %.8f | awk '{print $2, $1}' \
  | cs2cs -d 2 +proj=longlat +datum=WGS84 +to $OMERC
# 520000.02  999999.99    <- along flight lines: x only
# 499999.99  1020000.01   <- along tie lines:    y only

($OMERC is left unquoted on purpose: with +to, each + parameter has to be a separate argument.)

proj -V reports the scale factor and convergence at any point:

echo "-116.0 40.7" | proj -V $OMERC
# ...
# Meridian scale (h) : 1.00000549
# Convergence : 30d9'46.891" [ 30.16302521 ]

4. Export WKT

Most GIS software prefers WKT. Ask projinfo for WKT2 for QGIS and GDAL, and for ESRI WKT1 if you need a shapefile .prj that ArcGIS reads:

projinfo -q -o WKT2_2019 "$OMERC" > survey.wkt
projinfo -q -o WKT1_ESRI "$OMERC" > survey.prj
# GDAL equivalent: gdalsrsinfo -o wkt_esri survey.wkt

The WKT2 output names the method and both angles explicitly:

METHOD["Hotine Oblique Mercator (variant B)", ID["EPSG",9815]],
PARAMETER["Latitude of projection center",40.5, ...],
PARAMETER["Longitude of projection center",-116.25, ...],
PARAMETER["Azimuth of initial line",0, ...],
PARAMETER["Angle from Rectified to Skew Grid",-30, ...],
PARAMETER["Scale factor on initial line",1, ...],
PARAMETER["Easting at projection center",500000, ...],
PARAMETER["Northing at projection center",1000000, ...]

ESRI calls the same thing Rectified_Skew_Orthomorphic_Center and calls 𝛾𝑐 XY_Plane_Rotation:

PROJECTION["Rectified_Skew_Orthomorphic_Center"],
PARAMETER["False_Easting",500000.0],PARAMETER["False_Northing",1000000.0],
PARAMETER["Scale_Factor",1.0],PARAMETER["Azimuth",0.0],
PARAMETER["Longitude_Of_Center",-116.25],PARAMETER["Latitude_Of_Center",40.5],
PARAMETER["XY_Plane_Rotation",-30.0]

5. Move data into it

# point / line data (x y z), from UTM
cs2cs -d 2 EPSG:32611 "$(cat survey.wkt)" < lines_utm.xyz > lines_rot.xyz

# vectors
ogr2ogr -t_srs survey.wkt outline_rot.shp outline_utm.shp

# rasters
gdalwarp -t_srs survey.wkt -tr 100 100 -r bilinear tmi_utm.tif tmi_rot.tif

cs2cs accepts a WKT string but not a file name, hence the "$(cat survey.wkt)" [3].

6. Register it in QGIS

Open Settings › Custom Projections, choose WKT as the format, paste the contents of survey.wkt, and click Validate. QGIS recommends storing custom definitions as WKT rather than PROJ strings [4]. The CRS is saved in your QGIS user database, not in the project, so send the .wkt file along with the data when you share it. Then restart QGIS.

Miscellaneous Remarks

Magnetic declination is measured from true north. Reduction to the pole and other inclination/declination-dependent filters need the declination relative to the grid 𝑦 axis,

𝐷grid=𝐷true−𝑐,

where 𝑐 is the convergence (the azimuth of grid north) reported by proj -V. In a grid rotated by 𝜃, 𝑐≈𝜃 at the center, so a declination of +11° becomes 11°−30°=−19° in the example. Inclination does not change. UTM has convergence too (0.49° at my test center), so the same correction applies, just smaller.

Survey reports often give flight-line directions in UTM grid azimuth, not true azimuth. At 0.49° convergence, a line at 120° grid is at about 120.5° true. Over a 20 km line the difference is about 170 m sideways.

Running gdalwarp on an existing UTM grid interpolates it a second time and smooths short wavelengths. Where you can, transform the located line data (step 5) and grid it directly in the rotated CRS.
